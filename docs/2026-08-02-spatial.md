# Spatial structure nearly closes the gap — the first mechanism that helps

**Date:** 2026-08-02 · **Code:** `sim/carryover.js` (`positions`, `forageRange`),
`experiments/spatial.js` · Roadmap item B, fifth step.

Four mechanisms have failed and each failure sharpened the target. Placement selection converges;
inheritance blends; hybrids do pay 19.1%; a second pollinator gives no rare advantage; flower
constancy makes the penalty _worse_. The barrier is not that a novel placement is badly built — it is
that `rare/common` per-capita mating success sits near 0.26 because **there is nobody to exchange
pollen with.**

Constancy and pollinator heterogeneity both failed for the same reason: they give a rare morph more
**visits**, not more **partners**. Spatial structure is the one candidate that supplies partners —
with limited dispersal a new morph's offspring land beside it, so it is **locally common while
globally rare**, and a locally foraging bee moving between neighbours carries its pollen to its own
kind.

Plants sit at evenly spaced positions on a **ring** (a ring, not a line, so no plant is disadvantaged
by sitting at an edge) and the bee's next visit is drawn from a Gaussian kernel around the plant it
is standing on.

## ⚠️ It has to be an interaction, and that is the whole test

Clustering with a globally foraging bee should do nothing — the bee ignores the arrangement. Local
foraging over a scattered population should do nothing either — there is no patch to exploit. A
mechanism that "works" in three of four cells is a knob. So the full 2×2:

```
  layout        foraging     rare/common
  scattered     global         0.148
  scattered     local          0.048
  clustered     global         0.142
  clustered     local          0.360     <- 2.4x the best other cell
```

**Confirmed.** Only the combination pays, and each factor _alone_ is worse than doing neither —
local foraging over a scattered population is actively harmful (0.048), because the bee gets trapped
among the wrong neighbours.

## How tight must foraging be?

The rare patch spans 4/24 of the ring = 0.167, so a kernel much wider than that should behave
globally and one much narrower should stay inside the patch.

```
  forage range   rare/common
        global   0.247
          0.25   0.265
          0.12   0.381
          0.06   0.558
          0.03   0.796
         0.015   0.950
```

Monotone, and it tracks the patch width as predicted. **`rare/common` rises from 0.25 to 0.95** — a
roughly fourfold relief of the mate-finding penalty, and the first mechanism in five to move it in
the right direction at all.

## What is _not_ claimed

**Parity is not an advantage.** 0.950 raw is ~0.876 after correcting for the bias below, and
disruptive selection needs `rare/common > 1`, not ≈ 1. Spatial structure **relieves** the penalty; it
has not been shown to reverse it.

⚠️ **A real bias in exactly the cell of interest.** The calibration control — an identical morph,
where there is nothing for arrangement or foraging to act on — scores 1.012, 1.021, 1.013 in three
cells and **1.084** in clustered+local. That survived seed-averaging, so it is bias rather than
noise, and every clustered+local figure should be read ~8% lower than printed.

⚠️ **The patch-growing arm is under-powered and is not a result.** Holding frequency at 1/6 and
growing the patch gives corrected values 0.889, 0.800, **1.220** — non-monotone, one site-set draw
per cell. The 12/72 cell crossing 1.0 is a hint about where to look next, not a finding, and it is
not claimed as one.

⚠️ **And the measurement needed fixing mid-experiment.** The first run used a single bout seed and
produced a control column of 0.729 / 1.231 / 1.139 — where it must be ~1.00 — alongside a focal value
of 2.149. This project had already learned that lesson in the dispersal-unit work and recorded it;
it was not applied here until the numbers were visibly impossible. Everything above is now averaged
over five bout seeds.

## Where roadmap B stands

Five mechanisms, four clean negatives and one partial positive:

| mechanism                | effect on `rare/common`     |
| ------------------------ | --------------------------- |
| pollinator heterogeneity | 0.258 → 0.325, no advantage |
| flower constancy         | 0.296 → **0.198**, worse    |
| spatial structure        | 0.247 → **0.950**           |

Spatial structure is the mechanism, and the remaining question is sharp: **does a larger patch push
it past 1.0?** The under-powered arm above hints yes and the mechanism argues yes — a bigger patch
has more un-emptied partners, which is the separate partner-availability effect already measured in
the constancy work (baseline 0.471 → 0.550 as the rare morph grew at fixed frequency). That is the
next experiment, and it needs many more draws than this one had.

## Limits

One bee, one continuous bout, plants on a 1-D ring at even spacing. A 2-D landscape with clumped
rather than evenly spaced plants would be more realistic and might behave differently.

Clustering here is imposed, not emergent — there is no dispersal process producing the patch. That is
the honest gap between this and a real limited-dispersal model: it shows what spatial structure
_would_ buy, not that the structure arises.

No demography. This is instantaneous frequency dependence, not a demonstration that a polymorphism
would be protected across generations.
